# Core Analysis

## 1. Total Revenue

Revenue was calculated from:

```text
FactResellerSales[SalesAmount]
```

SQL logic:

```sql
SUM(SalesAmount)
```

This represents the sales generated by each salesperson.

---

## 2. Total Profit

Profit was calculated as:

```text
Profit = SalesAmount - TotalProductCost
```

SQL logic:

```sql
SUM(SalesAmount) - SUM(TotalProductCost)
```

This allows salesperson performance to be evaluated on profitability as well as revenue.

---

## 3. Profit Margin

Profit Margin was calculated as:

```text
Profit Margin =
Total Profit / Total Revenue
```

The metric was expressed as a percentage.

Negative profit margins were retained because they represent a real calculated business result under the selected sales and cost definitions and should not be removed simply because they are negative.

---

## 4. Total Orders

Orders were counted using distinct sales orders:

```sql
COUNT(DISTINCT SalesOrderNumber)
```

This avoids double counting because `FactResellerSales` contains transaction-level records and a single order can contain multiple sales lines.

---

## 5. Average Order Value

Average Order Value was calculated as:

```text
AOV =
Total Revenue / Distinct Orders
```

The distinct order count was used as the denominator rather than the number of fact rows.

---

## 6. Year-over-Year Growth

Year-over-year growth was calculated using yearly salesperson sales:

```text
YoY Growth % =
(Current Year Sales - Previous Year Sales)
/
Previous Year Sales
× 100
```

SQL Server window functions such as `LAG()` were used to obtain the previous-year sales value.

### Important interpretation

Negative growth values are valid.

For example:

```text
-17.81%
```

means that sales decreased by 17.81% relative to the comparison year.

Very large positive growth values were also retained when mathematically valid.

For example, one salesperson had approximately:

```text
2012 Sales = 49,824.71
2013 Sales = 1,371,986.21
YoY Growth = 2,653.63%
```

The unusually high percentage is explained by the small previous-year base. The value was therefore retained but interpreted with context rather than treated as a data error.

# SQL Analysis

The main salesperson performance analysis combined the fact table with employee and territory dimensions.

A representative SQL pattern was:

```sql
SELECT
    e.EmployeeKey,
    e.FirstName + ' ' + e.LastName AS Salesperson,
    t.SalesTerritoryRegion AS Territory,

    SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS TotalSales,

    SUM(f.SalesAmount) - SUM(f.TotalProductCost) AS TotalProfit,

    (
        SUM(f.SalesAmount) - SUM(f.TotalProductCost)
    ) * 100.0
    / NULLIF(SUM(f.SalesAmount), 0) AS ProfitMarginPct,

    COUNT(DISTINCT f.SalesOrderNumber) AS TotalOrders,

    SUM(f.SalesAmount) * 1.0
    / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT f.SalesOrderNumber), 0)
        AS AverageOrderValue

FROM dbo.FactResellerSales AS f

INNER JOIN dbo.DimEmployee AS e
    ON f.EmployeeKey = e.EmployeeKey

INNER JOIN dbo.DimSalesTerritory AS t
    ON f.SalesTerritoryKey = t.SalesTerritoryKey

WHERE e.SalesPersonFlag = 1

GROUP BY
    e.EmployeeKey,
    e.FirstName,
    e.LastName,
    t.SalesTerritoryRegion;
```

The exact ranking and growth queries were built on top of the validated salesperson-level results.

# Key Findings From the Analysis

The analysis demonstrated several important principles.

### Revenue alone is insufficient

Revenue alone does not measure the full quality of salesperson performance because a salesperson can generate high sales while producing low profit margins or declining growth.

### Negative growth can be meaningful

A negative YoY percentage indicates declining sales compared with the previous-year comparison period and should be interpreted as a performance signal rather than a data-quality error.

### Very high growth requires context

A very high growth percentage can occur when the previous-year sales base is small. Therefore, both current sales and the growth percentage should be considered together.

### Profitability matters

Negative profit margins indicate that sales and cost relationships should be examined rather than relying on revenue alone.

### Territory affects comparison

Sales opportunities can vary by territory, so salesperson performance should be interpreted alongside territory information.

# Recommendations Framework

The final recommendation should consider a combination of:

```text
Revenue
Profit
Profit Margin
Growth
AOV
Quota/Target Attainment
Territory
```

Examples of recommendation logic:

### High Revenue + High Margin

Potential benchmark salesperson whose practices may be studied and shared.

### High Revenue + Low/Negative Margin

Investigate pricing, product mix, discounts, and cost structure.

### High Growth + Low Prior-Year Base

Recognize strong momentum, but avoid interpreting the growth percentage without looking at absolute sales.

### High Margin + Low Revenue

Investigate opportunities to increase volume while preserving profitability.

### Negative Growth

Review territory, account mix, order activity, and previous-year baseline before drawing conclusions.

# Interview Question 1

## Why is revenue alone insufficient?

Revenue alone does not measure the full quality of salesperson performance because a salesperson can generate high sales while producing low profit margins or declining growth. A balanced evaluation should consider revenue together with profit, profitability, growth, average order value, quota attainment, and territory context. This provides a more reliable assessment of both sales volume and business value.

---

# Interview Question 2

## How can territory affect comparison?

Territory can affect salesperson comparison because different territories may contain different market sizes, customer opportunities, product demand, and sales potential. Therefore, directly comparing revenue across territories may not always represent fair performance. Including territory in the analysis allows salespeople to be compared within their business context and helps management interpret differences more accurately.
